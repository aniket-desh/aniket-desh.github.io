# From the forge to frozen disorder

A blacksmith shaping a steel blade has to control how it cools as carefully as how it is hammered. After heating the metal, the smith plunges it into water or oil, cooling it fast enough to change its internal structure. With the right steel and heat treatment, the blade emerges harder. Two pieces with the same chemical composition can consequently have different mechanical properties because they were cooled differently. The history of the forge survives in the arrangement of the atoms. [Cambridge hardenability practical](https://www.phase-trans.msm.cam.ac.uk/2000/practicals/AP3/AP3.html).

Carbon makes this possible. Steel contains carbon atoms that can occupy spaces between the much larger iron atoms. At the temperatures used for hardening, carbon dissolves in a crystalline phase of iron called austenite. During slower cooling, it has time to redistribute as the material transforms into phases that accommodate carbon differently. A sufficiently rapid quench suppresses these diffusional transformations. The iron lattice can instead change through coordinated atomic displacements into martensite, carrying the carbon into a structure that retains more of it than would remain dissolved at equilibrium.[^steel] [Bhadeshia, *Ferrous Alloys*](https://www.phase-trans.msm.cam.ac.uk/2002/MP10-6.pdf).

The trapped carbon strains the lattice and interferes with the motion of dislocations, the defects through which a crystal can deform. This contributes to the hardness of the quenched steel. The transformation also generates a microstructure with its own defects and internal stresses, so the final mechanical properties depend on how the carbon and the crystal structure developed together. A smith controls those properties partly by controlling how much time these processes have to occur. [Bhadeshia, *Ferrous Alloys*](https://www.phase-trans.msm.cam.ac.uk/2002/MP10-6.pdf), [*Tempered Martensite*](https://www.phase-trans.msm.cam.ac.uk/2004/Tempered.Martensite/tempered.martensite.html).

Statistical physics borrows the words *quenched* and *annealed* from these heat treatments. Annealing gives a material time and thermal mobility to rearrange toward a more favorable structure. Quenching can preserve features of its preparation by making further rearrangement too slow to matter during an observation. This gives the word *frozen* a precise, relative meaning. A variable is quenched when it stays effectively fixed over the timescale on which we study the other variables. [Mézard and Montanari, section 5.4](https://web.stanford.edu/~montanar/RESEARCH/BOOK/partB.pdf).

We can see the distinction more directly in a dilute magnetic alloy, such as copper containing a small fraction of manganese. The manganese atoms occupy an irregular collection of sites in the solid and carry magnetic moments associated with their electrons. Once the alloy has been prepared and cooled, the atoms can remain at essentially the same sites throughout an experiment, while their magnetic moments change orientation. We can therefore study magnetic fluctuations within a persistent arrangement of atoms. [Nieuwenhuizen and van Duin](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/9804143).

That arrangement matters because the interaction between two magnetic moments depends on their separation and can favor either parallel or antiparallel alignment. An irregular distribution of magnetic atoms produces a corresponding pattern of competing interactions. As a moment changes direction, it responds to the interactions established by the atoms around it. Some arrangements make the preferences of different pairs incompatible, a property called *frustration*. The magnetic system has to respond collectively to constraints imposed by the frozen spatial disorder. [Edwards's account of the spin-glass problem](https://garfield.library.upenn.edu/classics1985/A1985APZ5700001.pdf).

There are now two distinct sources of variation. Within one piece of alloy, the moments fluctuate in a particular arrangement of atoms. Across separately prepared pieces, that arrangement varies too. A theory of quenched disorder respects this order. It first studies the magnetic system with the atomic positions held fixed, then asks how its properties vary across possible realizations of those positions. Treating the positions as additional equilibrating variables would describe an annealed ensemble, in which the magnetic configuration and the arrangement of atoms can adjust together.[^timescales] [Mézard and Montanari, section 5.4](https://web.stanford.edu/~montanar/RESEARCH/BOOK/partB.pdf).

The randomness in this description expresses which details of the sample we control and which we leave unspecified. We may know the concentration of manganese while representing its microscopic placement statistically. Each prepared sample nevertheless has a definite arrangement, and all of its magnetic fluctuations occur within that same arrangement. This gives us a probability distribution that depends on another random object, the frozen sample itself. Calculating properties of such distributions is the problem that will lead us to replicas. [Zavatone-Veth, introduction to the replica method](https://jzv.io/assets/pdf/wishart_eigenvalue_and_gaussian_data_pca_replica_notes.pdf).

The same distinction appears when we study a neural network. A dataset can stay fixed while parameters explore a posterior distribution, or a set of weights can stay fixed while we vary the inputs. In either case, we must specify which variables define the instance and which variables we allow to fluctuate within it. The metallurgy supplies the vocabulary, and the magnetic alloy makes the statistical structure explicit. We can carry that structure into learning once we have chosen what plays each role. [Winer and Hanin](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.23982v2), [Zavatone-Veth's notes](https://jzv.io/notes).

[^steel]: The blade remains solid during this hardening treatment, and austenite and martensite are crystalline phases. Carbon is an alloying constituent whose concentration is deliberately controlled in steel. The word *impurity* in a statistical model can refer more broadly to an atom differing from the host species. After quenching, steel is often tempered by reheating it below the temperature at which austenite forms. Carbon redistribution and changes to the defect structure can then improve toughness at the expense of some hardness. [Bhadeshia, *Tempered Martensite*](https://www.phase-trans.msm.cam.ac.uk/2004/Tempered.Martensite/tempered.martensite.html).

[^timescales]: The separation between positional and magnetic relaxation depends on temperature and observation time. In a spin-glass regime, collective magnetic relaxation can become very slow, and measurements can retain a dependence on preparation history. An equilibrium calculation at fixed atomic positions therefore requires attention to whether the magnetic variables have actually equilibrated on the available timescale. [Dasgupta's spin-glass lectures](https://www.icts.res.in/sites/default/files/1267685100Chandan-Dasgupta-Jan8-Lecture1.pdf).

<summary>Sidebar — a frustrated triangle</summary>

Consider three magnetic moments constrained to point either up or down, with every pair preferring opposite orientations. If the first points up and the second down, their interaction is satisfied. To satisfy its interaction with the second, the third must point up, leaving it aligned with the first. Every possible assignment leaves at least one pair unsatisfied. This is the simplest example of frustration. The Ising constraint makes the contradiction particularly transparent, while the moments in an actual alloy can have more complicated orientational freedom. Frustration supplies a local example of competing preferences. Establishing a spin-glass phase or replica symmetry breaking requires a further analysis of the collective system. [Dasgupta's spin-glass lectures](https://www.icts.res.in/sites/default/files/1267685100Chandan-Dasgupta-Jan8-Lecture1.pdf), [Binder and Young](https://doi.org/10.1103/RevModPhys.58.801).
