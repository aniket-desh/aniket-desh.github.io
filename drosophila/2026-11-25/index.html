The bias–variance tradeoff in ridge regression

Suppose your training data follow the linear model \[ y=X\theta_*+\varepsilon,\qquad \mathbb E[\varepsilon]=0,\quad \operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2 I_n, \] where \(X\in\mathbb R^{n\times p}\) is fixed. For \(\lambda>0\), define the ridge estimator \[ \hat\theta_\lambda=\arg\min_{\theta\in\mathbb R^p}\left\{\|y-X\theta\|_2^2+\lambda\|\theta\|_2^2\right\}. \] Starting from this optimization problem, derive the expected squared prediction error at a fixed new input \(x\in\mathbb R^p\), \[ \mathbb E_\varepsilon\!\left[\bigl(x^\top\hat\theta_\lambda-x^\top\theta_*\bigr)^2\right]. \]

Since we want to understand how the training noise \(\varepsilon\) changes the prediction \(x^\top\hat\theta_\lambda\), we note that \(X\), \(x\), and \(\theta_*\) are fixed, so all randomness comes from \(\varepsilon\).

We will first solve the optimization problem, then separate the prediction error into a deterministic bias and a random fluctuation.

First, expand the objective \[ \begin{aligned} L(\theta) &=\|y-X\theta\|_2^2+\lambda\|\theta\|_2^2\\ &=(y-X\theta)^\top(y-X\theta)+\lambda\theta^\top\theta\\ &=y^\top y-y^\top X\theta-\theta^\top X^\top y +\theta^\top X^\top X\theta+\lambda\theta^\top\theta. \end{aligned} \] The two middle terms are equal because they are transposes of the same scalar: \[ y^\top X\theta=\theta^\top X^\top y. \] Therefore, \[ L(\theta) =y^\top y-2\theta^\top X^\top y +\theta^\top(X^\top X+\lambda I_p)\theta. \]

Differentiate with respect to \(\theta\). The constant term contributes zero, the linear term contributes \(-2X^\top y\), and the quadratic term contributes \(2(X^\top X+\lambda I_p)\theta\), since its matrix is symmetric: \[ \nabla_\theta L =-2X^\top y+2(X^\top X+\lambda I_p)\theta. \] Setting the gradient to zero gives \[ \begin{aligned} -2X^\top y+2(X^\top X+\lambda I_p)\hat\theta_\lambda&=0,\\ 2(X^\top X+\lambda I_p)\hat\theta_\lambda&=2X^\top y,\\ (X^\top X+\lambda I_p)\hat\theta_\lambda&=X^\top y. \end{aligned} \]

To justify taking the inverse, let \(v\neq0\). Then \[ \begin{aligned} v^\top(X^\top X+\lambda I_p)v &=v^\top X^\top Xv+\lambda v^\top v\\ &=(Xv)^\top(Xv)+\lambda\|v\|_2^2\\ &=\|Xv\|_2^2+\lambda\|v\|_2^2\\ &>0, \end{aligned} \] because \(\lambda>0\). Thus \(X^\top X+\lambda I_p\) is positive definite and invertible. The Hessian is twice this matrix, so the stationary point is the unique minimum: \[ \boxed{\hat\theta_\lambda=(X^\top X+\lambda I_p)^{-1}X^\top y.} \]

For readability, define \[ G:=X^\top X, \qquad A_\lambda:=(G+\lambda I_p)^{-1}. \] Both \(G\) and \(A_\lambda\) are symmetric. In particular, \[ A_\lambda^\top =\bigl((G+\lambda I_p)^\top\bigr)^{-1} =(G+\lambda I_p)^{-1} =A_\lambda. \]

Now substitute the data-generating model \(y=X\theta_*+\varepsilon\): \[ \begin{aligned} \hat\theta_\lambda &=A_\lambda X^\top(X\theta_*+\varepsilon)\\ &=A_\lambda X^\top X\theta_*+A_\lambda X^\top\varepsilon\\ &=A_\lambda G\theta_*+A_\lambda X^\top\varepsilon. \end{aligned} \]

The useful identity is \[ \begin{aligned} A_\lambda G &=A_\lambda\bigl[(G+\lambda I_p)-\lambda I_p\bigr]\\ &=A_\lambda(G+\lambda I_p)-\lambda A_\lambda\\ &=I_p-\lambda A_\lambda. \end{aligned} \] Using it, \[ \begin{aligned} \hat\theta_\lambda &=(I_p-\lambda A_\lambda)\theta_*+A_\lambda X^\top\varepsilon\\ &=\theta_*-\lambda A_\lambda\theta_*+A_\lambda X^\top\varepsilon. \end{aligned} \] Subtract \(\theta_*\): \[ \boxed{ \hat\theta_\lambda-\theta_* =-\lambda A_\lambda\theta_*+A_\lambda X^\top\varepsilon. } \]

This already separates two effects. The first term is the systematic displacement caused by regularization. The second is the training noise after it passes through the estimator.

When we take expectations over the training noise \(\varepsilon\), we get \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E_\varepsilon[\hat\theta_\lambda] &=\theta_*-\lambda A_\lambda\theta_* +A_\lambda X^\top\mathbb E_\varepsilon[\varepsilon]\\ &=\theta_*-\lambda A_\lambda\theta_*, \end{aligned} \] since \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon]=0\).

At the new input \(x\), the prediction bias is therefore \[ \begin{aligned} b_\lambda(x) &:=\mathbb E_\varepsilon[x^\top\hat\theta_\lambda]-x^\top\theta_*\\ &=x^\top\mathbb E_\varepsilon[\hat\theta_\lambda]-x^\top\theta_*\\ &=x^\top(\theta_*-\lambda A_\lambda\theta_*)-x^\top\theta_*\\ &=-\lambda x^\top A_\lambda\theta_*. \end{aligned} \] Consequently, \[ \boxed{b_\lambda(x)^2=\lambda^2(x^\top A_\lambda\theta_*)^2.} \]

To obtain the expected squared error, multiply the parameter-error equation by \(x^\top\): \[ x^\top\hat\theta_\lambda-x^\top\theta_* =-\lambda x^\top A_\lambda\theta_* +x^\top A_\lambda X^\top\varepsilon. \] Write its random part as \[ z_\lambda(x):=x^\top A_\lambda X^\top\varepsilon. \] Its mean is zero: \[ \mathbb E_\varepsilon[z_\lambda(x)] =x^\top A_\lambda X^\top\mathbb E_\varepsilon[\varepsilon] =0. \]

The prediction error is \(b_\lambda(x)+z_\lambda(x)\). Expanding its square gives \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E_\varepsilon\!\left[ (x^\top\hat\theta_\lambda-x^\top\theta_*)^2 \right] &=\mathbb E_\varepsilon[(b_\lambda(x)+z_\lambda(x))^2]\\ &=\mathbb E_\varepsilon[ b_\lambda(x)^2+2b_\lambda(x)z_\lambda(x)+z_\lambda(x)^2]\\ &=b_\lambda(x)^2 +2b_\lambda(x)\mathbb E_\varepsilon[z_\lambda(x)] +\mathbb E_\varepsilon[z_\lambda(x)^2]\\ &=b_\lambda(x)^2+\mathbb E_\varepsilon[z_\lambda(x)^2]. \end{aligned} \] The cross term vanishes because the random fluctuation has mean zero.

It remains to evaluate \(\mathbb E[z_\lambda(x)^2]\). First, the noise assumptions imply \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon) &=\mathbb E[\varepsilon\varepsilon^\top] -\mathbb E[\varepsilon]\mathbb E[\varepsilon]^\top\\ &=\mathbb E[\varepsilon\varepsilon^\top], \end{aligned} \] so \[ \mathbb E[\varepsilon\varepsilon^\top]=\sigma^2 I_n. \]

Because \(z_\lambda(x)\) is a scalar, its square equals its product with its transpose: \[ \begin{aligned} z_\lambda(x)^2 &=(x^\top A_\lambda X^\top\varepsilon) (x^\top A_\lambda X^\top\varepsilon)^\top\\ &=(x^\top A_\lambda X^\top\varepsilon) (\varepsilon^\top X A_\lambda^\top x)\\ &=x^\top A_\lambda X^\top \varepsilon\varepsilon^\top X A_\lambda x. \end{aligned} \] Taking expectations, \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E_\varepsilon[z_\lambda(x)^2] &=x^\top A_\lambda X^\top \mathbb E_\varepsilon[\varepsilon\varepsilon^\top] X A_\lambda x\\ &=x^\top A_\lambda X^\top(\sigma^2 I_n)X A_\lambda x\\ &=\sigma^2 x^\top A_\lambda X^\top X A_\lambda x\\ &=\sigma^2 x^\top A_\lambda G A_\lambda x. \end{aligned} \] This is exactly the prediction variance, since \[ x^\top\hat\theta_\lambda -\mathbb E_\varepsilon[x^\top\hat\theta_\lambda] =z_\lambda(x). \]

Putting everything together, \[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb E_\varepsilon\!\left[ \bigl(x^\top\hat\theta_\lambda-x^\top\theta_*\bigr)^2 \right]\\[2mm] &\quad= \underbrace{ \lambda^2\left[x^\top(X^\top X+\lambda I_p)^{-1}\theta_*\right]^2 }_{\text{squared prediction bias}} \\[2mm] &\qquad\quad+ \underbrace{ \sigma^2x^\top(X^\top X+\lambda I_p)^{-1} X^\top X (X^\top X+\lambda I_p)^{-1}x }_{\text{prediction variance}}. \end{aligned} } \]

To see why this represents a tradeoff, consider a unit eigenvector \(u_j\) of \(G=X^\top X\), with eigenvalue \(d_j\geq0\). Since \[ (G+\lambda I_p)u_j=(d_j+\lambda)u_j, \qquad A_\lambda u_j=\frac{1}{d_j+\lambda}u_j, \] the expected fitted coefficient in this direction is \[ \begin{aligned} u_j^\top\mathbb E[\hat\theta_\lambda] &=u_j^\top(I_p-\lambda A_\lambda)\theta_*\\ &=\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{d_j+\lambda}\right)u_j^\top\theta_*\\ &=\frac{d_j}{d_j+\lambda}u_j^\top\theta_*. \end{aligned} \] Thus ridge shrinks each signal component by \(d_j/(d_j+\lambda)\).

For prediction specifically at \(x=u_j\), the two error contributions become \[ \text{bias}^2 =\left(\frac{\lambda}{d_j+\lambda}\right)^2 (u_j^\top\theta_*)^2, \qquad \text{variance} =\frac{\sigma^2d_j}{(d_j+\lambda)^2}. \] For \(d_j>0\), increasing \(\lambda\) increases the shrinkage bias in that direction while decreasing the variance. For general \(x\), bias contributions from different directions can cancel, so squared bias need not increase monotonically.

Finally, there is no additional \(\sigma^2\) term in the requested answer: the target is the noiseless quantity \(x^\top\theta_*\). Predicting a fresh noisy response \(y_{\rm new}=x^\top\theta_*+\varepsilon_{\rm new}\), with independent noise of variance \(\sigma^2\), would add \(\sigma^2\).
