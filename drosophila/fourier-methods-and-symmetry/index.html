Fourier methods and symmetry

Aniket Deshpande · Published September 26, 2026 · Updated September 26, 2026

Fourier analysis expresses a function in modes that behave simply under symmetry. On a circle, these modes are the familiar complex exponentials einθe^{in\theta}einθ. On a sphere, they are spherical harmonics. When an operator respects the relevant symmetry, these modes can also be its eigenfunctions, turning an integral or differential equation into separate equations for individual coefficients.

This is why an eigendecomposition on a symmetric domain can sometimes be understood as a Fourier decomposition. The important condition is that the operator and the integration measure must respect the symmetry. The geometry of the domain alone is insufficient.

Fourier methods also appear in statistical mechanics when we represent a constraint using a Dirac delta function. The underlying mathematics is closely related, but the variable being Fourier-expanded is different. We will develop both applications.

The Fourier modes diagonalize TTT because TTT respects the rotations that define those modes.

For an arbitrary function,

An integral operation has become multiplication of each coefficient by a scalar. Changing κ\kappaκ changes the eigenvalues, while the Fourier basis continues to work.

The discrete version is a circulant matrix: a matrix whose entries depend only on the difference between indices, modulo the number of sites. Its eigenvectors form the discrete Fourier basis.

There is already a simple example in the one-dimensional Ising model. At zero external field, the transfer matrix is

T=(eβJe−βJe−βJeβJ).\mathsf T = \begin{pmatrix} e^{\beta J}&e^{-\beta J}\\ e^{-\beta J}&e^{\beta J} \end{pmatrix}.T=(eβJe−βJ​e−βJeβJ​).

Its normalized eigenvectors are

v+=12(11),v−=12(1−1),\mathbf v_+ = \frac1{\sqrt2} \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf v_- = \frac1{\sqrt2} \begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix},v+​=2​1​(11​),v−​=2​1​(1−1​),

with eigenvalues

λ+=2cosh⁡(βJ),λ−=2sinh⁡(βJ).\lambda_+=2\cosh(\beta J), \qquad \lambda_-=2\sinh(\beta J).λ+​=2cosh(βJ),λ−​=2sinh(βJ).

These are the Fourier modes of the two-element group Z2\mathbb Z_2Z2​. To see this, label the spin states by s=(−1)bs=(-1)^bs=(−1)b, where b∈{0,1}b\in\{0,1\}b∈{0,1}. The two discrete Fourier functions are

e2πi(0)b/2=1,e2πi(1)b/2=(−1)b=s.e^{2\pi i(0)b/2}=1, \qquad e^{2\pi i(1)b/2}=(-1)^b=s.e2πi(0)b/2=1,e2πi(1)b/2=(−1)b=s.

One is even under spin flip, and the other is odd. The transfer matrix preserves these symmetry sectors, so it acts on each by multiplication by a scalar. The familiar diagonalization of this 2×22\times22×2 matrix is therefore a small Fourier calculation.

We can now replace the circle by the ordinary unit sphere,

S2={x∈R3:∥x∥=1}.S^2=\{\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^3:\|\mathbf x\|=1\}.S2={x∈R3:∥x∥=1}.

ℓ=0,1,2,…,m=−ℓ,…,ℓ.\ell=0,1,2,\ldots, \qquad m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell.ℓ=0,1,2,…,m=−ℓ,…,ℓ.

They are the angular functions encountered in quantum mechanics. Their relation to ordinary Fourier modes is visible in the eigenvalue equations

Here, ΔS2\Delta_{S^2}ΔS2​ is the Laplacian intrinsic to the sphere. Both bases organize functions by their spatial variation, measured through a Laplacian eigenvalue.

Suppose a kernel depends only on the angle between its arguments:

K(x,x′)=κ(x⋅x′).K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x') = \kappa(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf x').K(x,x′)=κ(x⋅x′).

(TKf)(x)=∫S2κ(x⋅x′)f(x′) dμ(x′).(T_Kf)(\mathbf x) = \int_{S^2} \kappa(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf x')f(\mathbf x')\,d\mu(\mathbf x').(TK​f)(x)=∫S2​κ(x⋅x′)f(x′)dμ(x′).

The spherical counterpart of the convolution calculation is the Funk–Hecke theorem:

where PℓP_\ellPℓ​ is the Legendre polynomial normalized by Pℓ(1)=1P_\ell(1)=1Pℓ​(1)=1. Angular integration has reduced the eigenvalue calculation to a one-dimensional integral.

Every mmm at the same degree ℓ\ellℓ has the same eigenvalue. Rotations generally mix the 2ℓ+12\ell+12ℓ+1 harmonics within that degree, so an operator respecting all rotations cannot distinguish a preferred orientation among them.

More precisely, each fixed-ℓ\ellℓ space is an irreducible rotation representation: it cannot be split into smaller nonzero subspaces preserved by every rotation. Each such representation occurs once in scalar functions on S2S^2S2, and Schur’s lemma makes a rotation-invariant operator scalar on it. For more general spaces, symmetry may only reduce an operator to blocks.

For a sufficiently regular kernel, we can consequently write

K(x,x′)=∑ℓ,mλℓYℓm(x)Yℓm(x′)∗.K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x') = \sum_{\ell,m} \lambda_\ell Y_{\ell m}(\mathbf x) Y_{\ell m}(\mathbf x')^*.K(x,x′)=ℓ,m∑​λℓ​Yℓm​(x)Yℓm​(x′)∗.

This is the kernel’s spectral decomposition, expressed in a basis supplied by spherical geometry.

The kernel operator acts on an entire function space and identifies which kinds of functions it can represent.

The spectrum also becomes useful for studying learning dynamics. Assume a fixed positive-semidefinite kernel and population gradient flow for squared-error regression:

∂tft(x)=−∫K(x,x′)[ft(x′)−f∗(x′)] dμ(x′).\partial_t f_t(\mathbf x) = -\int K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x') \big[f_t(\mathbf x')-f_*(\mathbf x')\big]\,d\mu(\mathbf x').∂t​ft​(x)=−∫K(x,x′)[ft​(x′)−f∗​(x′)]dμ(x′).

Each harmonic component relaxes independently. A larger eigenvalue means faster learning; a zero eigenvalue means that component of the initial error persists. This mode-wise description also appears in the fixed-kernel limit of neural-network training.

Whether lower-degree features learn faster depends on the eigenvalues of the particular kernel. Symmetry identifies the modes but does not, by itself, order their learning rates.

These statements concern a population operator. With finitely many randomly sampled inputs, the empirical measure generally breaks exact rotational symmetry, and the eigenvectors of the sample kernel matrix need not be exact sampled spherical harmonics. Likewise, when feature learning changes the kernel during training, a single fixed spectral decomposition need not describe the full dynamics.

A second application of Fourier analysis appears when a calculation contains a hard constraint. Consider weights w∈RN\mathbf w\in\mathbb R^Nw∈RN restricted by

1N∑j=1Nwj2=1.\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_j^2=1.N1​j=1∑N​wj2​=1.

We can impose the constraint inside an integral using

Z=∫RNdNw δ ⁣(u(w))e−βH(w).Z = \int_{\mathbb R^N}d^Nw\, \delta\!\big(u(\mathbf w)\big)e^{-\beta H(\mathbf w)}.Z=∫RN​dNwδ(u(w))e−βH(w).

The delta restricts the integration to the sphere of radius N\sqrt NN​, with a measure proportional to its surface measure.

On the real line, our Fourier-transform convention is

δ~(k)=∫Rδ(u)e−iku du=1.\widetilde\delta(k) = \int_{\mathbb R}\delta(u)e^{-iku}\,du = 1.δ(k)=∫R​δ(u)e−ikudu=1.

This is a distributional identity: it holds after integration against a suitable test function. Intuitively, combining all frequencies produces a spike at zero.

There is a direct mathematical connection between these constraint representations and the earlier spectral decompositions. A delta distribution is the kernel of the identity operator. On the real line,

Every Fourier mode has eigenvalue one under the identity.

On the sphere, the corresponding completeness relation is

In JZV’s constraint calculation, however, the Fourier variable kkk is conjugate to the scalar violation uuu. It does not label angular variation around the weight sphere. This is why the delta-function technique works even when the Hamiltonian has no rotational symmetry: the Fourier expansion takes place in the constraint variable.
