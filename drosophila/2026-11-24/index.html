Noether’s theorem for a Galilean boost

Take particles of masses \(m_i\) with \(L=\sum_i m_i|\dot{\mathbf r}_i|^2/2-V(\{\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j\})\), where \(V\) depends only on relative positions. Under an infinitesimal boost \(\mathbf r_i(t)\mapsto\mathbf r_i(t)+\boldsymbol\epsilon t\), show that the change in \(L\) is a total time derivative. Starting from the first variation of the action, derive the associated conserved vector and explain what its conservation says about the center of mass. Don’t assume Noether’s theorem or momentum conservation.

November 24, 2026

We have particles with constant masses \(m_i>0\), positions \(\mathbf r_i(t)\), and Lagrangian \[ L=\sum_i\frac{m_i}{2}|\dot{\mathbf r}_i|^2 -V\bigl(\{\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j\}\bigr). \] Assume the physical trajectories are twice continuously differentiable.

The boost changes \(L\) by a total derivative

Introduce a dimensionless parameter \(\lambda\) to track the infinitesimal boost: \[ \mathbf r_i^{(\lambda)}(t) =\mathbf r_i(t)+\lambda\boldsymbol\epsilon t, \] where \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) is a constant vector with units of velocity. A first variation means differentiation with respect to \(\lambda\) at \(\lambda=0\).

The time coordinate is unchanged. Differentiating the boosted position with respect to time, \[ \dot{\mathbf r}_i^{(\lambda)} =\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\mathbf r_i+\lambda\boldsymbol\epsilon t\bigr) =\dot{\mathbf r}_i+\lambda\boldsymbol\epsilon, \] so \[ \delta\mathbf r_i \equiv\left.\frac{\partial\mathbf r_i^{(\lambda)}}{\partial\lambda}\right|_0 =\boldsymbol\epsilon t, \qquad \delta\dot{\mathbf r}_i =\boldsymbol\epsilon. \]

Physically, every particle receives the same additional velocity, so at each instant every particle is displaced by the same amount \(\lambda\boldsymbol\epsilon t\). Their relative positions therefore remain unchanged: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf r_i^{(\lambda)}-\mathbf r_j^{(\lambda)} &=\mathbf r_i+\lambda\boldsymbol\epsilon t -\mathbf r_j-\lambda\boldsymbol\epsilon t\\ &=\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j. \end{aligned} \] Since \(V\) depends only on these differences, \[ V\bigl(\{\mathbf r_i^{(\lambda)}-\mathbf r_j^{(\lambda)}\}\bigr) =V\bigl(\{\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j\}\bigr), \qquad \delta V=0. \]

Only the kinetic energy changes. Expanding its squared velocity, \[ \begin{aligned} |\dot{\mathbf r}_i+\lambda\boldsymbol\epsilon|^2 &=(\dot{\mathbf r}_i+\lambda\boldsymbol\epsilon) \cdot(\dot{\mathbf r}_i+\lambda\boldsymbol\epsilon)\\ &=|\dot{\mathbf r}_i|^2 +\lambda\dot{\mathbf r}_i\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon +\lambda\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}_i +\lambda^2|\boldsymbol\epsilon|^2\\ &=|\dot{\mathbf r}_i|^2 +2\lambda\dot{\mathbf r}_i\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon +\lambda^2|\boldsymbol\epsilon|^2. \end{aligned} \] Consequently, \[ \begin{aligned} L^{(\lambda)}-L &=\sum_i\frac{m_i}{2} \left( 2\lambda\dot{\mathbf r}_i\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon +\lambda^2|\boldsymbol\epsilon|^2 \right)\\ &=\lambda\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon +\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\left(\sum_i m_i\right)|\boldsymbol\epsilon|^2. \end{aligned} \] Taking the derivative at \(\lambda=0\) gives the first variation: \[ \delta L =\left.\frac{\partial L^{(\lambda)}}{\partial\lambda}\right|_0 =\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon. \]

Because the masses and the boost vector are constant, \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt} \left(\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon\right) &=\sum_i m_i\frac{d\mathbf r_i}{dt}\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon\\ &=\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon. \end{aligned} \] Thus, defining \[ F=\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon, \] we have established \[ \boxed{\delta L=\frac{dF}{dt}.} \] This identity holds for any differentiable paths, before we impose equations of motion. It means the boost changes the action only through its endpoints: \[ \begin{aligned} \delta S &=\int_{t_a}^{t_b}\delta L\,dt\\ &=\int_{t_a}^{t_b}\frac{dF}{dt}\,dt\\ &=F(t_b)-F(t_a)\\ &=[F]_{t_a}^{t_b}. \end{aligned} \]

The first variation of the action

We now derive a second expression for \(\delta S\) from the variational principle. For this step, consider an arbitrary path variation, \[ \mathbf r_i^{(\lambda)} =\mathbf r_i+\lambda\,\delta\mathbf r_i, \] where \(\delta\mathbf r_i(t)\) need not be a boost. Its velocity variation satisfies \[ \begin{aligned} \delta\dot{\mathbf r}_i &=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda} \frac{d\mathbf r_i^{(\lambda)}}{dt}\right|_0\\ &=\frac{d}{dt} \left.\frac{\partial\mathbf r_i^{(\lambda)}}{\partial\lambda}\right|_0\\ &=\frac{d}{dt}\delta\mathbf r_i. \end{aligned} \] Applying the chain rule to \[ S=\int_{t_a}^{t_b}L\,dt \] therefore gives \[ \begin{aligned} \delta S &=\int_{t_a}^{t_b}\delta L\,dt\\ &=\int_{t_a}^{t_b}\sum_i \left[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf r_i}\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i +\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i} \cdot\delta\dot{\mathbf r}_i \right]dt\\ &=\int_{t_a}^{t_b}\sum_i \left[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf r_i}\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i +\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i} \cdot\frac{d}{dt}\delta\mathbf r_i \right]dt. \end{aligned} \]

To identify what stationarity requires inside the interval, we want every interior term to multiply \(\delta\mathbf r_i\) itself, not its derivative. The product rule gives \[ \frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i}\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i \right) = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i}\right) \cdot\delta\mathbf r_i + \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i} \cdot\frac{d}{dt}\delta\mathbf r_i. \] Rearranging, \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i} \cdot\frac{d}{dt}\delta\mathbf r_i = \frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i}\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i \right) - \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i}\right) \cdot\delta\mathbf r_i. \] Integrating this identity, \[ \begin{aligned} \int_{t_a}^{t_b} \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i} \cdot\frac{d}{dt}\delta\mathbf r_i\,dt &= \left[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i}\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i \right]_{t_a}^{t_b}\\ &\quad- \int_{t_a}^{t_b} \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i}\right) \cdot\delta\mathbf r_i\,dt. \end{aligned} \] Substituting back into \(\delta S\), summing over particles, and combining the interior integrals, \[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \delta S &= \left[ \sum_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i} \cdot\delta\mathbf r_i \right]_{t_a}^{t_b}\\ &\quad+ \int_{t_a}^{t_b}\sum_i \left[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf r_i} -\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i}\right) \right]\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i\,dt. \end{aligned} } \] This is an identity for the first variation; we have not yet used stationarity.

Equations of motion from fixed endpoints

The physical trajectory makes the action stationary under variations with fixed endpoints: \[ \delta\mathbf r_i(t_a)=\delta\mathbf r_i(t_b)=0. \] For these variations the boundary term vanishes, so \[ 0=\delta S =\int_{t_a}^{t_b}\sum_i \left[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf r_i} -\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i}\right) \right]\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i\,dt. \] Each particle’s variation can be chosen independently, in any direction, and localized around any interior time. Since the bracket is continuous for twice-differentiable trajectories, the integral can vanish for every such choice only if \[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf r_i} -\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i}\right) =0 } \] for each \(i\). These are the Euler–Lagrange equations, obtained directly from stationarity.

Now return to the general first-variation identity. Along a trajectory satisfying these equations, its interior integral vanishes. Therefore, for any variation along that trajectory, \[ \delta S = \left[ \sum_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i} \cdot\delta\mathbf r_i \right]_{t_a}^{t_b}. \] The variation is now allowed to move the endpoints, so in particular we can use the boost.

This distinction matters. Fixed-endpoint variations established the equations of motion, but the boost generally moves the endpoints, so we must keep its boundary term and must not set its \(\delta S\) to zero.

The conserved vector

For our Lagrangian, \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}_i} =m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i. \] Explicitly, for a Cartesian component \(\alpha\), \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot r_{i\alpha}} &=\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot r_{i\alpha}} \left(\frac{m_i}{2}\sum_\beta\dot r_{i\beta}^{\,2}\right)\\ &=\frac{m_i}{2}\sum_\beta 2\dot r_{i\beta}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\\ &=m_i\dot r_{i\alpha}, \end{aligned} \] because the potential contains no velocities.

Substituting the boost variation \(\delta\mathbf r_i=\boldsymbol\epsilon t\) into the boundary expression gives \[ \begin{aligned} \delta S &=\left[ \sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i\cdot(\boldsymbol\epsilon t) \right]_{t_a}^{t_b}\\ &=\left[ \boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot \left(t\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i\right) \right]_{t_a}^{t_b}. \end{aligned} \] But the direct boost calculation gave \[ \delta S=[F]_{t_a}^{t_b} = \left[ \boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i \right]_{t_a}^{t_b}. \] These describe the same action variation. Equating them and subtracting, \[ \left[ \boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot \left( t\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i-\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i \right) \right]_{t_a}^{t_b}=0. \]

Define \[ \mathbf Q(t) =t\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i-\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i. \] Because \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) is constant, the endpoint relation becomes \[ \boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot \bigl(\mathbf Q(t_b)-\mathbf Q(t_a)\bigr)=0. \] The boost direction is arbitrary, and choosing it along each Cartesian axis shows that every component of the difference vanishes: \[ \mathbf Q(t_b)-\mathbf Q(t_a)=\mathbf 0. \] The endpoints can be any two times along the physical trajectory. Hence \[ \boxed{ \mathbf Q=t\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i-\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i \quad\text{is constant}. } \] One often uses the opposite sign for the boost charge: \[ \boxed{ \mathbf K=-\mathbf Q =\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i-t\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i. } \] Both conventions express the same conservation law.

What it says about the center of mass

To interpret the conserved vector physically, define the total mass and center of mass: \[ M=\sum_i m_i, \qquad \mathbf R=\frac{1}{M}\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i. \] Multiplying the second definition by \(M\), \[ M\mathbf R=\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i, \] and differentiating with constant masses, \[ M\dot{\mathbf R}=\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i. \] The conserved vector therefore becomes \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf Q &=tM\dot{\mathbf R}-M\mathbf R\\ &=M(t\dot{\mathbf R}-\mathbf R). \end{aligned} \]

Conservation means its derivative vanishes: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf 0 =\frac{d\mathbf Q}{dt} &=M\frac{d}{dt}(t\dot{\mathbf R}-\mathbf R)\\ &=M\left( \frac{dt}{dt}\dot{\mathbf R} +t\frac{d\dot{\mathbf R}}{dt} -\dot{\mathbf R} \right)\\ &=M\left( \dot{\mathbf R}+t\ddot{\mathbf R}-\dot{\mathbf R} \right)\\ &=Mt\ddot{\mathbf R}. \end{aligned} \] For \(t\neq0\), divide by \(Mt\): \[ \ddot{\mathbf R}=\mathbf 0. \] Since the trajectories are twice continuously differentiable, \(\ddot{\mathbf R}\) is continuous, so this also holds at \(t=0\). Thus the center of mass has zero acceleration throughout the trajectory.

Integrating once from any reference time \(t_0\), \[ \begin{aligned} \dot{\mathbf R}(t)-\dot{\mathbf R}(t_0) &=\int_{t_0}^{t}\ddot{\mathbf R}(s)\,ds\\ &=\int_{t_0}^{t}\mathbf 0\,ds\\ &=\mathbf 0. \end{aligned} \] Therefore \[ \dot{\mathbf R}(t)=\dot{\mathbf R}(t_0) \equiv\mathbf V_{\rm CM}, \] a constant vector. Integrating again, \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf R(t)-\mathbf R(t_0) &=\int_{t_0}^{t}\dot{\mathbf R}(s)\,ds\\ &=\int_{t_0}^{t}\mathbf V_{\rm CM}\,ds\\ &=\mathbf V_{\rm CM}(t-t_0). \end{aligned} \] Hence \[ \boxed{ \mathbf R(t)=\mathbf R(t_0)+\mathbf V_{\rm CM}(t-t_0). } \] The center of mass moves in a straight line at constant velocity, even while internal interactions produce complicated individual motions.

Writing this as \[ \mathbf R(t)=\mathbf R_0+\mathbf V_{\rm CM}t, \] where \(\mathbf R_0=\mathbf R(t_0)-\mathbf V_{\rm CM}t_0\), gives \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf Q &=M(t\dot{\mathbf R}-\mathbf R)\\ &=M\left[t\mathbf V_{\rm CM} -\left(\mathbf R_0+\mathbf V_{\rm CM}t\right)\right]\\ &=-M\mathbf R_0. \end{aligned} \] So the boost charge records the center-of-mass position extrapolated back to time zero: \[ \boxed{\mathbf K=M\mathbf R_0.} \]

Finally, total momentum conservation follows as a consequence: \[ \mathbf P\equiv\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i =M\dot{\mathbf R} =M\mathbf V_{\rm CM}, \] which is constant. We derived that result from the action rather than using it to establish the boost conservation law.
